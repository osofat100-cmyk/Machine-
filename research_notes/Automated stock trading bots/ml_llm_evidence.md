# Evidence that ML models and LLMs can profitably trade or predict US stocks (as of 23 November 2026)

Evidence-type tags used below: **[BACKTEST]** = historical simulation only; **[LIVE-SIM]** = live market data, simulated/paper money (or money type not stated); **[LIVE-REAL]** = real money reported; **[VENDOR/SECONDARY]** = figure comes from a company blog, press rewrite or third-party recomputation rather than the primary source; **[SELF-REPORTED]** = unaudited claim by the person or firm running the test.

## 1. ML return prediction in equities: what does the academic evidence show, net of costs? (Gu-Kelly-Xiu, Avramov et al., "virtue of complexity", intraday ML)

### Takeaway
ML does predict stock returns better than simple regressions in academic backtests, but the edge is tiny per stock (about 0.4% monthly out-of-sample R2), needs thousands of stocks, long-short portfolios and >100% monthly turnover to harvest, and most of the profit sits in microcaps, distressed and hard-to-trade names; published Sharpe ratios are before trading costs. After-cost intraday evidence exists mainly for the aggregate index/liquid ETFs or at millisecond horizons, not for a retail account trading single stocks every few minutes.

### Cited Findings
- **[BACKTEST] Scale of the benchmark study.** Gu, Kelly and Xiu (Review of Financial Studies 2020; NBER w25398) use "nearly 30,000 individual stocks over 60 years from 1957 to 2016", 94 stock characteristics and 900+ predictors once interactions are included; 18 years training (1957-1974), 12 years validation (1975-1986), 30 years out-of-sample test (1987-2016). — [NBER w25398](https://www.nber.org/papers/w25398.pdf)
- **[BACKTEST] The predictive edge is small.** Monthly stock-level out-of-sample R2: plain OLS on all predictors = -3.46% (worse than forecasting zero); OLS with only 3 predictors (size, value, momentum) = 0.16%; elastic net = 0.11%; PCR = 0.26%; PLS = 0.27%; random forest = 0.33%; boosted trees = 0.34%; neural nets 0.33% (NN1) peaking at 0.40% (NN3). — [NBER w25398](https://www.nber.org/papers/w25398.pdf)
- **[BACKTEST] Portfolio results before costs.** A value-weighted long-short decile portfolio on NN4 forecasts earns 2.3%/month with an annualized out-of-sample Sharpe of 1.35; equal-weighted Sharpe 2.45, falling to 1.69 once stocks below the 20th NYSE size percentile are dropped. Market-timing the S&P 500 with NN forecasts gives Sharpe 0.77 vs 0.51 for buy-and-hold. — [NBER w25398](https://www.nber.org/papers/w25398.pdf)
- **[BACKTEST] High turnover; costs not deducted.** Turnover for NN1-NN5 is "consistently between 110% and 130% per month"; the paper reports turnover rather than net-of-cost returns and notes the role of price-trend predictors explains the high turnover. — [NBER w25398](https://www.nber.org/papers/w25398.pdf)
- **What the models "see".** All methods agree on the same dominant signals: recent price trends (short-term reversal, 12-month momentum, momentum change, industry momentum, max return), liquidity (turnover, dollar volume, Amihud illiquidity, bid-ask spread, size), volatility/beta, then valuation ratios. — [NBER w25398](https://www.nber.org/papers/w25398.pdf)
- **[BACKTEST] Critique: profits concentrate in hard-to-trade stocks.** Avramov, Cheng and Metzker (Management Science 2023, 69(5): 2587-2619) find that excluding microcaps, distressed stocks or high-volatility episodes "considerably attenuates" ML profitability, and that "performance further deteriorates in the presence of reasonable trading costs due to high turnover and extreme positions". Deep-learning signals remain profitable in long positions and recent years. — [SSRN abstract](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=3450322); [Management Science](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/fpi/10.1287/mnsc.2022.4449)
- **[BACKTEST] Size of the attenuation.** On US stocks 1987-2017, ML strategies earned value-weighted risk-adjusted returns of 0.75%-1.88% per month; the payoff fell 62% excluding microcaps, 68% excluding non-rated firms and 80% excluding distressed firms around downgrades. Co-author Si Cheng: "An average investor would struggle to achieve alpha after taking transaction costs into account." — [CUHK Business School summary](https://cbk.bschool.cuhk.edu.hk/the-limitations-of-using-ai-to-pick-stocks/)
- **Nuance.** A practitioner summary notes traditional (non-ML) anomaly strategies were hurt by the same restrictions "by a similar proportional magnitude" — i.e. the restrictions bite on all cross-sectional strategies, not only ML. — [Advisor Perspectives](https://www.advisorperspectives.com/articles/2023/03/06/the-promise-of-machine-learning)
- **Results depend heavily on researcher choices.** Chen, Hanauer and Kalsbach test all combinations of seven design choices (1,056 ML models): gross long-short decile returns range from 0.13% to 1.98% per month and annualized Sharpe ratios from 0.08 to 1.82, before transaction costs. — [Robeco, Dec 2024](https://www.robeco.com/en-sg/insights/2024/12/better-by-design-why-human-choices-matter-for-return-predictions-via-machine-learning)
- **"Virtue of complexity" claim.** Kelly, Malamud and Zhou (Journal of Finance 2024) argue that models with more parameters than observations improve out-of-sample US equity market return prediction, and that simple models "severely understate" predictability. — [NBER w30217](https://www.nber.org/papers/w30217); [AQR](https://www.aqr.com/Insights/Research/Journal-Article/The-Virtue-of-Complexity-in-Return-Prediction)
- **Critique of the complexity claim.** Stefan Nagel (NBER w34104, Aug 2025) shows that with ~12,000 random features fitted on rolling windows as short as 12 months, the forecasts collapse to a weighted average of past returns — a volatility-timed momentum strategy; when he fed the method simulated data with reversals instead of momentum, it still built a momentum strategy (and performed poorly), so the success comes from the method's structure rather than learning from the data. — [NBER w34104](https://www.nber.org/papers/w34104)
- **Wider dispute.** Press coverage (Bloomberg, Aug 2025, seen here only via a blog repost) reports at least six papers challenging the complexity result, including Daniel Buncic (Stockholm Business School); Elmore and Strauss (Economics Letters) find added complexity does not outperform standard ridge regression. Kelly and Malamud responded in "Understanding the Virtue of Complexity" (SFI 2025). — [blog repost of Bloomberg coverage](https://climateerinvest.blogspot.com/2025/08/aqrs-hard-to-believe-study-spurs-clash.html); [Elmore & Strauss record](https://digitalcommons.du.edu/business_info_fac/6); [SFI response](https://www.sfi.ch/en/publications/n-25-96-understanding-the-virtue-of-complexity)
- **[BACKTEST] Intraday, index level, after costs.** Huddleston, Liu and Stentoft (Journal of Financial Econometrics 2023) use ML on 5-minute returns and report intraday *market* (index) returns are predictable beyond transaction costs, with ensemble models reaching Sharpe 0.98 after costs. — [RePEc record](https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/jfinec/v21y2023i2p485-527..html)
- **[BACKTEST] Intraday, liquid ETFs.** Aleti, Bollerslev and Siggaard (Management Science 2025) predict the intraday aggregate market return from high-frequency factor-zoo returns with regularized ML and report sizeable out-of-sample Sharpe ratios and alphas for liquid ETFs after costs. — [Management Science](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/epdf/10.1287/mnsc.2023.01657)
- **Ultra-high-frequency predictability is real but is a speed game.** Ait-Sahalia, Fan, Xue and Zhou find predictability in high-frequency returns is "large, systematic and pervasive over short horizons" and they value each millisecond of data timeliness. — [NBER w30366](https://www.nber.org/papers/w30366)
- **[BACKTEST] Single-stock intraday ML without costs.** Ghosh, Neufeld and Sahoo report daily returns of 0.64% (LSTM) and 0.54% (random forest) trading S&P 500 stocks open-to-close — before transaction costs. — [arXiv 2004.10178](https://arxiv.org/abs/2004.10178)
- **Practitioner view.** AQR's "Can Machines Learn Finance?" argues finance has structurally low signal-to-noise and short histories, and that ML is likelier to help first with risk management, transaction-cost analysis and portfolio construction than with new return predictions. Robeco researchers likewise note ML works best with large data and high signal-to-noise, "whereas financial research data sets are comparatively small and the signal-to-noise ratio tends to be low". — [Institutional Investor on AQR](https://www.institutionalinvestor.com/article/b1fsn64kfq8b5h/aqr-s-problem-with-machine-learning-cats-morph-into-dogs); [Robeco 2023](https://www.robeco.com/en-me/insights/2023/04/researchers-have-just-been-scratching-the-surface-of-ml-in-asset-management)

### Inferences
- "Can it see things we can't?" — in the best-known study the ML models mostly re-weighted and interacted *already-known* signals (momentum, reversal, liquidity, volatility). The gain came from combining ~900 weak inputs across ~30,000 stocks, not from discovering a hidden pattern in one price series.
- A 0.4% monthly R2 means the model is wrong about almost all of any single stock's move. It only becomes money when averaged across hundreds of long and short positions rebalanced monthly — a structure a $1,000 long-only account cannot replicate (no diversified short book, high turnover, microcap spreads).
- The after-cost intraday results are for the index/ETFs with institutional cost assumptions, or for millisecond horizons. They do not show that a minute-bar ML model on individual liquid stocks pays a retail trader after spread and slippage.
- The data requirement contrast is stark: GKX trained on 18+ years and tens of thousands of stocks; Nagel shows that a "complex" model fitted on 12 months of data learned nothing beyond momentum. A model fitted to a few months of one thin token (the reader's earlier experience) is far below the data scale at which ML has been shown to work.

### Gaps
- I found no peer-reviewed study showing minute-level ML prediction on individual liquid US stocks that is profitable after realistic retail costs. The search returned index/ETF-level and pre-cost single-stock results only; absence of a finding in my searches is not proof that none exists.
- GKX and most follow-ups do not publish net-of-cost Sharpe ratios; the exact after-cost Sharpe for a large-cap-only ML portfolio was not retrieved (Avramov et al.'s full tables were paywalled/403; figures above are from the abstract and a university summary).
- Real, audited live track records of ML return-prediction funds are generally not public; I found none that could be cited.
- The Bloomberg account of the "virtue of complexity" dispute was only accessible through a secondary repost.

## 2. Simple vs complex models, how much data is needed, and what backtest-overfitting research implies for someone trying thousands of settings

### Takeaway
A small, regularised linear model captures a large share of what deep models find (0.16%-0.27% vs 0.40% monthly R2), whereas an unregularised regression on many inputs is worse than guessing zero. The overfitting literature says that with about 5 years of data, testing as few as 45 strategy variants is enough to find one that looks good in-sample and fails out-of-sample — so a search over thousands of settings on a few months of data will essentially always produce a great-looking, worthless backtest.

### Cited Findings
- **Linear vs deep, same data.** In Gu-Kelly-Xiu: 3-variable OLS R2 0.16%/month; linear dimension-reduction (PCR/PLS) 0.26%/0.27%; best neural net 0.40%; OLS on all 900+ predictors -3.46%. The generalized linear model with splines but no interactions "fails to robustly outperform the linear specification". — [NBER w25398](https://www.nber.org/papers/w25398.pdf)
- **When non-linear beats linear.** Across 1,056 design combinations, non-linear models are more likely to beat linear ones only under certain choices (market-relative continuous target, expanding training window); ensembles generally beat individual algorithms. — [Robeco, Dec 2024](https://www.robeco.com/en-sg/insights/2024/12/better-by-design-why-human-choices-matter-for-return-predictions-via-machine-learning)
- **Complexity vs ridge.** Elmore and Strauss report that increasing complexity does not outperform standard ridge regression in the ridge setting. — [Elmore & Strauss record](https://digitalcommons.du.edu/business_info_fac/6)
- **Backtest overfitting — the 45-trial result.** Bailey, Borwein, Lopez de Prado and Zhu ("Pseudo-Mathematics and Financial Charlatanism", Notices of the AMS 2014): "for a model based on 5 years of data, one can be misled by looking at even as few as 45 sample configurations" — within that many variants at least one will show a good Sharpe ratio in-sample and a dismal one out-of-sample. Authors: "overfitting is pathological within the financial industry." — [AMS news release, 10 Apr 2014](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/817304); [Bailey slides](https://cdar.berkeley.edu/sites/default/files/dhb-risk-2017.pdf)
- **Probability of Backtest Overfitting (PBO).** The same authors propose combinatorially symmetric cross-validation to estimate the probability a selected backtest is overfit; "the higher the number of configurations tried, the greater is the probability that the backtest is overfit", and analysts rarely report the number of trials. — [SSRN 2326253](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=2326253)
- **Deflated Sharpe Ratio.** Bailey and Lopez de Prado (Journal of Portfolio Management 2014) correct a reported Sharpe ratio for selection bias under multiple testing and for non-normal returns (number of trials, skewness, kurtosis). — [SSRN 2460551](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=2460551)
- **ML always finds a pattern.** Lopez de Prado (then at AQR, 2018): ML algorithms "will always identify a pattern, even when none exists". — [Bloomberg, 9 Oct 2018](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2018-10-09/the-big-problem-with-machine-learning-algorithms)
- **Multiple testing in published research.** Harvey, Liu and Zhu ("...and the Cross-Section of Expected Returns") survey 313 papers on cross-sectional return patterns and conclude a newly discovered factor should clear a t-ratio above 3.0 rather than the usual ~2.0. — [NBER w20592](https://www.nber.org/papers/w20592)
- **Even genuine published signals decay.** McLean and Pontiff (Journal of Finance 2016) study 97 published predictors: returns are 26% lower out-of-sample and 58% lower post-publication (the CFA digest words these as "percentage points"; the paper is usually quoted as percent declines), implying roughly a 32-point further drop attributable to investors trading on the publication. — [CFA Institute digest](https://rpc.cfainstitute.org/research/cfa-digest/2016/06/does-academic-research-destroy-stock-return-predictability-digest-summary)
- **Institutions apply the same discipline to AI-generated signals.** Man Group describes repeated testing by its "AlphaGPT" agents as a multiple-testing ("p-hacking") problem that makes "lucky" results look good in research but fail live, and says the AI can "conceptualise one idea but implement something different". — [Man Group](https://www.man.com/insights/what-ai-can-do-for-alpha)

### Inferences
- For the reader: a linear/ridge regression is a reasonable *first* ML model — the evidence says it gets most of what is gettable — but only with few, pre-chosen inputs and regularisation. Feeding many indicators into plain linear regression is the worst case in GKX (negative R2).
- The reader's earlier failure (rule-based bots that shone in backtests then failed on unseen data) is exactly the outcome the Bailey et al. result predicts: thousands of settings on months of data is far beyond the ~45-trials-per-5-years threshold. Switching from rules to ML does not fix this; ML has *more* free parameters and overfits more easily.
- Practical implication: count every variant tried, hold out sealed data that is touched once, and deflate the backtest Sharpe for the number of trials. A backtest Sharpe that would be impressive for one pre-registered test is unremarkable as the best of 1,000.
- An LLM asked to "find a strategy" and iterate on backtests is itself a multiple-testing machine (Man Group flags this for its own system), so it speeds up overfitting unless trial counts are controlled.

### Gaps
- The exact Minimum Backtest Length formula was not retrieved in readable form (the PDF fetched was the PBO paper). From memory, unverified here: the bound is roughly 2*ln(N)/SR^2 years for N independent trials to avoid an expected-maximum in-sample Sharpe of SR arising from pure noise. The report writer should verify before quoting a formula.
- The statement "in-sample Sharpe 1.0 implies expected out-of-sample Sharpe zero beyond 45 trials" was found only in secondary write-ups, not confirmed in the paper text.
- No source gives a clean "you need X observations" rule for minute-bar data; minute bars are highly autocorrelated in regime, so more bars over the same few months do not add independent market regimes (this is my reasoning, not a cited fact).
- McLean-Pontiff percentages vs percentage points: wording differs between the digest and common citations; not checked against the journal text.

## 3. LLM evidence on stocks: Lopez-Lira & Tang and what happened out of sample; look-ahead / training-data leakage in LLM backtests

### Takeaway
The strongest LLM result is news classification: GPT-4 scoring of headlines predicted next-day drift out of sample (Sharpe ~3 before costs, Oct 2021-May 2024), but the edge lasted ~2 days, was strongest in small stocks and on the short side, was wiped out at 20 bps round-trip cost, and its Sharpe fell from 6.5 to 1.2 as LLM use spread. Separately, a growing literature shows that LLM trading *backtests* run inside the model's training window are inflated by memorisation; post-cutoff performance of published agents decays by roughly half or disappears.

### Cited Findings
- **[BACKTEST, post-knowledge-cutoff] Lopez-Lira & Tang, latest version (30 Oct 2025).** Headlines for 4,123 US common stocks, October 2021-May 2024 (period chosen to be after the model's training cutoff). GPT-4 matches the *initial* market reaction with ~90% portfolio-day hit rates, which the authors call "non-tradable". — [arXiv 2304.07619v6](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2304.07619v6)
- **Tradable part (the drift).** Hit rates 58% (overnight news) and 55% (intraday news); long-short mean return 0.34%/day (overnight) before costs; pre-cost annualized Sharpe 2.97 (overnight) and 2.63 (intraday). The short leg does most of the work: long leg 8 bps/day (Sharpe 0.78) vs short leg 26 bps/day (Sharpe 2.01). Prices absorb the news "in about two days" (34 bps on day 1, 19 bps on day 2, nothing after). — [arXiv 2304.07619v6](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2304.07619v6)
- **Costs.** Cumulative pre-cost return ~700% (Oct 2021-May 2024); >300% at 5 bps round-trip; >100% at 10 bps; "a transaction cost of 20 bps round-trip makes the strategy unprofitable". Turnover ~190% per day; at 10 bps the Sharpe falls from 2.97 to 1.29. Authors: exploiting this "is only feasible for market participants whose transaction costs are sufficiently low, such as market makers", and is "challenging for large or unsophisticated investors". — [arXiv 2304.07619v6](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2304.07619v6)
- **Decay over time.** "Its annualized Sharpe ratio drops from 6.54 in 2021Q4 to 3.68 in 2022, 2.33 in 2023, and to 1.22 over January-May 2024"; abstract: "Strategy returns decline as LLM adoption rises, consistent with improved price efficiency." — [arXiv 2304.07619v6](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2304.07619v6)
- **Model size matters for this text task.** Drift-strategy Sharpe: GPT-4 2.97, GPT-3.5 1.66, DistilBART-MNLI 1.26, and negative for most basic models (GPT-1, GPT-2, BERT); a finance-tuned small model gets 90% on initial reaction but 48% on drift (Sharpe -0.33). — [arXiv 2304.07619v6](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2304.07619v6)
- **Not only small stocks, before costs.** Removing stocks priced at or below $5 and below the 20th NYSE size percentile still leaves >300% cumulative pre-cost return. — [arXiv 2304.07619v6](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2304.07619v6)
- **Look-ahead vs "distraction".** Glasserman and Lin separate look-ahead bias (the model knows what happened after a headline) from a distraction effect (general knowledge of the company muddies the sentiment call); in-sample, anonymised headlines performed better. — [arXiv 2309.17322](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.17322)
- **Formal test of memorisation (Dec 2025).** Gao, Jiang and Yan (CUHK) build a "Lookahead Propensity" measure. On 91,361 Bloomberg headlines for 1,587 firms (2012-2023, Llama-3.3-70B), the headline-return coefficient is 0.210 (t=12.24) alone, but once interacted with the memorisation measure the standalone LLM coefficient becomes insignificant (0.00117) and the interaction is 2.866 (t=4.86); out-of-sample placebo (Llama-2, Sep 2023-Dec 2024, 9,942 obs) shows no such interaction. Conclusion: "the predictive power of LLMs partly reflects memorization rather than genuine inference." — [arXiv 2512.23847](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.23847v1)
- **LLMs recall market data from before their cutoff.** Lopez-Lira, Tang and Zhu ("The Memorization Problem", 2025, revised 2026) show LLMs can reproduce exact values of key economic variables and roughly track index levels from before the training cutoff, with no recall after it; they conclude pre-cutoff "forecasting" tests cannot separate skill from memory. — [arXiv 2504.14765](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.14765v2)
- **[VENDOR/SECONDARY] Recall error figure.** A vendor article citing that paper reports GPT-4o's recall error for S&P 500 levels at 0.61% before its cutoff vs 16.87% after. — [Thor/Phoenix blog, 13 Nov 2026](https://thortradecopier.com/blog/can-chatgpt-trade-for-you)
- **Agent backtests decay after the cutoff.** "Profit Mirage" (arXiv, Oct 2025): LLM trading agents' strong backtest returns fall apart once the test window moves past the model's training cutoff; TradingAgents showed a 55.68% Sharpe decay and the best-performing agent also dropped ~50%. Authors: such agents "are not trading; they are regurgitating history". — [arXiv 2510.07920](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.07920v1)
- **[BACKTEST] Long-horizon re-test of published LLM agents (FINSABER, accepted KDD 2026).** In the original papers' narrow setup (4 stocks, Oct 2022-Apr 2023) FinMem looked strong (e.g. TSLA reported Sharpe 2.68 vs buy-and-hold -0.34). Over 2004-2024 on historical S&P 500 constituents including delisted names, buy-and-hold beat both LLM agents on Sharpe in every selection setup — e.g. random-five: buy-and-hold Sharpe 0.315 (6.7%/yr) vs FinMem -0.253 (-0.1%/yr) and FinAgent 0.094 (4.5%/yr); CAPM alpha not significant for either agent (all p > 0.34). — [arXiv 2505.07078v4](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.07078v4)
- **Regime behaviour.** Same study: buy-and-hold Sharpe 0.61 in bull years and -0.28 in bear years; FinAgent 0.12 / -0.38; FinMem -0.19 / -0.97 — the agents are "too cautious when risk is rewarded and too aggressive when it is penalised." Conclusion: "LLM-derived alpha is likely a methodological artefact of narrow, biased evaluations." Costs modelled at $0.0049/share with $0.99 minimum per order; long-only. — [arXiv 2505.07078v4](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.07078v4)
- **Original agent claims rest on very short windows.** TradingAgents' own evaluation is a backtest from 1 January to 29 March 2024 on a handful of large-cap tech stocks. — [arXiv 2412.20138](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2412.20138)
- **Leakage-controlled benchmark (May 2026, China A-shares, not US).** KTD-Fin masks tickers and dates for 10 frontier LLMs (incl. gpt-5.5, claude-opus-4-7, gemini-3.1-pro) over 2024-01-01 to 2026-04-10: five beat CSI300 buy-and-hold (+36.92%) on total return, but attribution shows returns "largely explained by passive market and style exposure"; only one model had stock-selection alpha at about zero (+0.2%) and nine were negative. — [arXiv 2605.28359](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.28359)
- **Counter-evidence: leakage is not the whole story for news.** He, Lv, Manela and Wu train "chronologically consistent" models (ChronoBERT/ChronoGPT) that only see text available at each date; they find look-ahead bias in news-based return prediction is modest, and a follow-up reports at least 54% of observed news-return predictability persists without leakage. — [arXiv 2502.21206](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2502.21206); [arXiv 2510.11677](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.11677v1)
- **Fundamentals.** Kim, Muhn and Nikolaev report GPT-4 predicting the direction of future earnings from anonymised financial statements with 60.4% accuracy, similar to a purpose-trained neural net and above the 53-57% range cited for human analysts. — [arXiv 2407.17866](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.17866v2)

### Inferences
- The one robust, post-cutoff LLM result is *reading text* (is this headline good or bad news for this firm?), not forecasting from price numbers. Even there, about half the measured in-sample effect in other studies may be memorisation (37% amplification in Gao et al.; "at least 54% persists" in He et al. — the two bracket roughly the same picture).
- The Lopez-Lira & Tang edge is not accessible to a $1,000 retail account as published: it needs a diversified long-short book across thousands of stocks, most of the return is on the short side, turnover is ~190%/day, and it dies at 20 bps round-trip. Its Sharpe had already fallen ~80% by early 2024; the sample ends May 2024, so its 2025-2026 performance is unknown.
- Any LLM-agent backtest whose test dates fall before the model's knowledge cutoff should be treated as contaminated by default. For a current frontier model that rules out essentially all history the reader could backtest on; only forward (paper) testing is clean.

### Gaps
- No published update of the Lopez-Lira & Tang strategy beyond May 2024 was found; whether any drift remains in 2025-2026 is not known.
- No independent replication or formal critique of Kim-Muhn-Nikolaev was found in this search.
- KTD-Fin is China A-shares; no equally rigorous leakage-controlled attribution study on US stocks was found.
- Sarkar & Vafa ("Lookahead Bias in Pretrained Language Models") was located only via citations, not read.

## 4. Live LLM trading benchmarks and experiments through November 2026: results versus buy-and-hold

### Takeaway
Across every live or forward test found, LLM agents show no consistent edge over buy-and-hold: in the only real-money test on US stock exposure (Alpha Arena Season 1.5, 2 weeks, leveraged), roughly 5-6 of 32 accounts made money and the group lost about a third of its capital; in simulated-money live benchmarks on unleveraged US stocks the models clustered within a few points of the index over 5-10 weeks, with between 0 and half beating it. All tests are far too short (2-10 weeks) for statistical conclusions, and no public LLM trader has a multi-year live record.

### Cited Findings
- **[LIVE-REAL] Alpha Arena Season 1 (Nof1), crypto not stocks.** 17/18 Oct-3 Nov 2025; six LLMs, $10,000 each, crypto perpetuals on Hyperliquid. Qwen3 Max finished ~$12,231 and DeepSeek ~$10,489; the other four lost. Per Nof1's end-of-season chart as relayed by a vendor article: Claude -30.81%, Grok -45.30%, Gemini -56.71%, GPT-5 -62.66%; total $60,000 fell to $43,172.29 (-28.05%). Press reports quote different final balances for the four losers (e.g. Claude $5,799, Gemini $5,445, Grok $4,208, GPT-5 $4,126) because the live board was captured at different moments; ranking is the same. — [ForkLog, 8 Dec 2025](https://forklog.com/en/ai-model-grok-4-2-triumphs-in-trading-tournament/); [Thor/Phoenix blog (vendor)](https://thortradecopier.com/blog/can-chatgpt-trade-for-you); [TradeRank (vendor)](https://www.traderank.ai/alpha-arena-leaderboard)
- **Nof1 founder on Season 1.** Jay Azhang: "LLMs don't really handle numerical time series data very well, but that's all the context we gave them." — [Thor/Phoenix blog quoting Azhang (vendor)](https://thortradecopier.com/blog/can-chatgpt-trade-for-you)
- **[LIVE-REAL] Alpha Arena Season 1.5, US stocks.** About two weeks, 19 Nov-3 Dec 2025 (founder announced the end on 5 Dec); eight models x four rule-sets (New Baseline, Monk Mode, Situational Awareness, Max Leverage) = 32 accounts of $10,000 ($320,000). Founder: "Mystery Model (a.k.a GROK 4.20) is the winner, up 12% on avg ... it made money in all four competitions"; GPT-5.1 second, Gemini 3 third. — [ForkLog](https://forklog.com/en/ai-model-grok-4-2-triumphs-in-trading-tournament/)
- **Season 1.5 aggregate.** Bloomberg (6 May 2026, as summarised by secondary outlets): across the four competitions the models "collectively lost roughly a third of their capital" and "only six of 32 outcomes ended in profit". - In unleveraged, long-only US stock benchmarks during a rising market, LLM agents mostly behaved like a slightly-under-invested index holder: positive, low drawdown, a few points either side of the benchmark. - When given leverage and freedom to trade often (Alpha Arena), most models lost heavily and fees alone consumed a double-digit share of capital in two weeks. The single winner in Season 1.5 was one unreleased model over two weeks — one draw from a noisy distribution, with no benchmark published.
- "Who won" changes from test to test (Qwen/DeepSeek in crypto S1; Grok 4.20 in S1.5; GPT-4.1 in LiveTradeBench; MiniMax in AI-Trader; Qwen in HKU FX). Inconsistent winners over short windows is what luck looks like.
- None of these benchmarks tested the reader's intended setup: a cheap model polling a single small account every few minutes on US cash equities under PDT/settlement constraints.

- Rallies.ai and other consumer "AI arenas" appeared in search results but were not examined; treat as untested here.

## 5. Do cheap/small LLMs perform differently from frontier models?

### Takeaway
For autonomous trading decisions, model size or leaderboard rank does not predict results — in live benchmarks cheap and mid-tier models finish above and below flagship ones more or less at random, and "reasoning" variants show no advantage. For text classification (headline sentiment, event tagging), bigger models were clearly better in the Lopez-Lira & Tang data, but small fine-tuned classifiers are competitive on standard sentiment benchmarks.

### Cited Findings
- **No link between general capability and trading result.** LiveTradeBench: Spearman correlation between LMArena score and cumulative return on US stocks = 0.054 (and -0.38 on Polymarket); reasoning models (DeepSeek-R1, Qwen3-235B-Thinking, GPT-o3) do not outperform. In its table, older/cheaper models such as GPT-4.1 (6.25%), Qwen2.5-72B (5.15%) and Llama4-Scout (4.65%) beat Claude-Opus-4.1 (3.73%), Gemini-2.5-Pro (1.95%) and Grok-4 (4.30%). — [arXiv 2511.03628](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.03628v1)
- **Cheaper models at the top of a live-sim US test.** AI-Trader: MiniMax-M2 (+9.56%) and DeepSeek-v3.1 (+8.39%) beat GPT-5 (+1.56%); Gemini-2.5-Flash was last (-0.06%). — [arXiv 2512.10971](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.10971v1)
- **But the smallest open models were worst in a replay test.** StockBench: GPT-OSS-20B -2.8% and GPT-OSS-120B -0.9% were the only agents below zero and below buy-and-hold (+0.4%) by a clear margin; Qwen3-30B-Think (+2.1%) was mid-table; GPT-5 (+0.3%) was below most open models. Reasoning tuning "does not consistently help". — [arXiv 2510.02209v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.02209v2)
- **Architecture over backbone.** Agent Market Arena concludes agent design matters more than which LLM is plugged in (though one framework swung from -38.72% to +21.91% on TSLA across backbones, so the claim is not uniform). — [arXiv 2510.11695](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.11695v2)
- **Scale is not the barrier.** FINSABER (FinMem tested with GPT-4o-mini and GPT-4o): "the primary barrier to successful LLM investors is not model scale, but a lack of domain-aware financial logic." — [arXiv 2505.07078v4](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.07078v4)
- **A "Lite" model in the top three of a live FX test.** HKU Agentic Trader: Seed-2.0-Lite finished in the leading tier while Claude Opus 4.6 was among the biggest losers (six weeks, FX). — [HKU report](https://www.hkubs.hku.hk/aimodelrankings_en/report/En_Agentic%20Trader%20Short%20Report.pdf)
- **For reading news, size helped.** Lopez-Lira & Tang: drift Sharpe 2.97 (GPT-4) vs 1.66 (GPT-3.5) vs 1.26 (DistilBART-MNLI) vs negative (GPT-1/GPT-2/BERT); GPT-4 beats GPT-3.5 by 12.2 bps on initial reaction and 4.9 bps on drift overall; Llama2-70B beats Llama2-7B by 58.6 bps overall on initial reaction and by 315.7 bps on earnings announcements — scale helps most "for tiny models". — [arXiv 2304.07619v6](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2304.07619v6)
- **For plain sentiment labels, small tuned models hold up.** On Financial PhraseBank: GPT-4o 0.85 accuracy zero-shot / 0.86 few-shot vs 0.88 for fine-tuned FinBERT; GPT-3.5-turbo 0.77-0.78. Another preprint finds fine-tuned small models (FinBERT, FinDRoBERTa) can outperform GPT-3.5/GPT-4 zero-shot on financial-news sentiment. — [arXiv 2410.01987](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2410.01987); [arXiv 2409.11408](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2409.11408)
- **Newer is not more consistent.** Wang & Wang: "more advanced models do not consistently demonstrate better consistency and reproducibility" across finance tasks (GPT-3.5-turbo, GPT-4o-mini, GPT-4o; 50 runs each). — [arXiv 2503.16974](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.16974)

### Inferences
- If the LLM's job is "decide trades", paying for a frontier model buys nothing demonstrable; the benchmarks show no ranking stability by price or size. If the job is "read and label text", a cheap current model is adequate for simple sentiment, with larger models better on nuanced items (earnings, rating changes) per Lopez-Lira & Tang.
- A cheap model is therefore a sensible choice for the tasks where LLMs have evidence (section 6), and no model is a sensible choice for the task where they do not (unsupervised price prediction).

### Gaps
- No benchmark directly compares cost per decision against trading outcome; "cheap" here is inferred from model tier, not from measured API spend.
- The Lopez-Lira & Tang size comparison uses 2021-2024 models; I found no equivalent comparison of 2026 small models (which may exceed 2023's GPT-4) on the same task.
- No study was found comparing small vs frontier models specifically at minute-level polling frequency.

## 6. Where does an LLM plausibly help a small trader (classifying/explaining/checking) versus predicting the next price move from numbers?

### Takeaway
The evidence supports LLMs as readers and assistants — classifying news, filings and earnings items, detecting events, summarising, and reviewing a bot's logic — where they are accurate, repeatable and cheap; it does not support them as predictors of the next price move from numerical data, where both the benchmark operator's own statements and live results are negative.

### Cited Findings
- **Reading news correctly.** GPT-4 identified the direction of the market's initial reaction to headlines with ~90% portfolio-day hit rates on post-cutoff data, across simple and complex headlines and press releases vs articles. Bigger models read harder items better: GPT-4 beat GPT-3.5 most on insider-transaction news (25.2 bps initial reaction, 13.8 bps drift) and by 15.4 bps on earnings/revenue, partnership and clinical-trial announcements, with no difference on dividend news (0.2 bps); within the Llama2 family the 70B model beat the 7B model by 315.7 bps on earnings announcements, 139.6 bps on rating changes, 85.0 bps on index inclusions and 59.7 bps on clinical-trial news (initial reaction). — [arXiv 2304.07619v6](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2304.07619v6)
- **But the tradable remainder is small and short-lived.** After the initial (non-tradable) jump, the drift lasts ~2 days and averages 34 bps/day before costs on a long-short book; intraday news must be acted on within ~15 minutes of release. — [arXiv 2304.07619v6](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2304.07619v6)
- **Fundamental reading.** GPT-4 predicted earnings direction from anonymised statements with 60.4% accuracy, on par with a specialised neural net. — [arXiv 2407.17866](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.17866v2)
- **Classification is repeatable.** Over 50 repeated runs, sentiment analysis reached Fleiss' kappa > 0.94 and binary classification > 0.93; multi-class classification of FOMC statements 0.86-0.91; numerical earnings predictions were less stable; averaging 3-5 runs "dramatically" improves consistency; LLMs were more consistent than expert human annotators. — [arXiv 2503.16974](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.16974)
- **Numbers are the weak spot.** Nof1's founder on why models lost: "LLMs don't really handle numerical time series data very well". StockBench logs arithmetic errors (wrong share quantities) and schema errors (invalid JSON) in agent outputs. — [Thor/Phoenix blog quoting Azhang (vendor)](https://thortradecopier.com/blog/can-chatgpt-trade-for-you); [arXiv 2510.02209v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.02209v2)
- **Knowledge does not transfer to trading.** StockBench's authors conclude strong results on static financial question-answering do not reliably carry over to trading; AI-Trader: "General intelligence ... does not automatically translate into effective trading capability." — [arXiv 2510.02209v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.02209v2); [arXiv 2512.10971](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.10971v1)
- **How institutions actually use it.** Large firms (JPMorgan, Balyasny) are described as using AI for research, fraud detection and internal analysis rather than handing over investment decisions; Man Group uses agents to generate, code and evaluate signal ideas under the same statistical tests as human research, with human sign-off. — [ZeroHedge summary of Bloomberg](https://www.zerohedge.com/markets/wall-street-keeps-testing-ai-traders-most-are-still-underperforming); [Man Group](https://www.man.com/insights/what-ai-can-do-for-alpha)
- **ML's better-evidenced uses are also "around" the prediction.** AQR expects ML to help first with risk management, transaction-cost analysis and portfolio construction rather than new return forecasts. — [Institutional Investor on AQR](https://www.institutionalinvestor.com/article/b1fsn64kfq8b5h/aqr-s-problem-with-machine-learning-cats-morph-into-dogs)

### Inferences
- Plausible value for a $1,000 trader, ranked by evidence: (1) event/news filter — "is there fresh, material, negative news on a stock my bot is about to buy?" used as a veto or pause, which exploits the well-evidenced reading skill and the finding that negative news drifts more; (2) summarising filings/earnings so a human decides faster; (3) explaining positions and writing a decision log; (4) reviewing the rule-based bot's code and assumptions for look-ahead, survivorship and multiple-testing errors; (5) generating hypotheses to be tested once on sealed data.
- Not supported: asking the LLM every few minutes "given these candles, will price go up?" Price bars every few minutes contain almost no new text for the model to read; that is the regime (numeric time series) where operators say LLMs are weak and where live tests lost money through overtrading.
- A news-veto role is also robust to the LLM being wrong: a false alarm costs a skipped trade, not capital. An LLM placing orders has unbounded downside from one bad output.
- Polling frequency should follow information arrival (a new headline, filing or earnings release), not the clock.

### Gaps
- No study was found that tests the "LLM as veto/filter on a rule-based retail bot" design directly; the recommendation is an inference from component evidence, not a measured result.
- No evidence was found on whether LLM code/assumption review measurably reduces backtest-overfitting errors.
- Whether headline reading still yields any after-cost drift for a retail trader in 2025-2026 is unknown (data ends May 2024).

## 7. Pitfalls specific to LLMs in trading: knowledge-cutoff look-ahead, nondeterminism, hallucinated figures, prompt injection through news, latency and cost

### Takeaway
LLM trading has failure modes that rule-based bots do not: backtests are contaminated by the model's memory of history, identical prompts produce different trades, models make arithmetic and format errors, and they can be steered by fabricated or doctored news (fake headlines succeeded 62%-100% of the time in a 2026 test). Frequent polling mainly multiplies fees and overtrading.

### Cited Findings
- **Knowledge-cutoff look-ahead.** LLMs can recall exact pre-cutoff economic values and index paths, with no recall post-cutoff; agent backtests lose ~50% or more of their Sharpe once tested after the cutoff; in a formal test the LLM's standalone predictive coefficient became insignificant once memorisation was controlled. — [arXiv 2504.14765](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.14765v2); [arXiv 2510.07920](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.07920v1); [arXiv 2512.23847](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.23847v1)
- **Narrow-window and survivorship bias in agent papers.** Claimed LLM-agent outperformance on 4 stocks over 6 months disappears over 20 years with delisted stocks included. — [arXiv 2505.07078v4](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.07078v4)
- **Nondeterminism in decisions.** Bloomberg: models "make wildly different decisions when given identical instructions"; under one prompt, trade counts ranged from 158 to 1,418 in a single two-week contest; StockBench had to average three seeded runs per model, and reports GPT-OSS-120B's return as -0.9% in one table and -1.2% in another. — [ZeroHedge summary of Bloomberg](https://www.zerohedge.com/markets/wall-street-keeps-testing-ai-traders-most-are-still-underperforming); [arXiv 2510.02209v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.02209v2)
- **Nondeterminism is task-dependent.** Simple classification is highly reproducible (kappa > 0.93) while numerical predictions are less stable; the paper warns of "G-hacking" — selectively reporting favourable runs. — [arXiv 2503.16974](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.16974)
- **Wrong numbers and broken outputs.** StockBench records arithmetic errors (incorrect share quantities) and schema errors (invalid JSON); thinking models made fewer arithmetic but more schema errors. Man Group: the AI "might conceptualise one idea but implement something different". — [arXiv 2510.02209v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.02209v2); [Man Group](https://www.man.com/insights/what-ai-can-do-for-alpha)
- **Fabricated news beats prompt injection (Oct 2026).** "Fake News, Real Crashes" (~10,000 decisions, 15 US stocks, 5 injection vectors): direct prompt injection succeeded 14% of the time, fabricated news 62%; two 30-word "fact-poisoning" templates succeeded on all 15 stocks (100%); removing surface credibility cues (named source, "BREAKING" tag) cut success from 100% to 0% with the fake claim unchanged; attacks transferred across model vendors and most evaded standard defences. — [arXiv 2610.26438](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.26438)
- **Invisible manipulation of headlines (Jan 2026).** Unicode homoglyph swaps caused the model to mis-handle 99% of adversarial headlines and hidden HTML text 67%; one day of adversarial news cut a 14-month simulation's profitability by ~3%. (Authors' own simulation.) — [arXiv 2601.13082](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.13082v1)
- **Agent-level injection.** TradeTrap: a reverse-expectation injection cut an agent's total return from 7.81% to 0.89% and Sharpe from 5.72 to 0.29. — [arXiv 2512.02261](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2512.02261)
- **Overtrading and fee drag.** Models "trade too much"; Alpha Arena Season 1.5 accounts paid an estimated 12%-16.5% of capital in fees in two weeks (third-party estimates); in HKU's FX test the most active model lost the most; FinMem's commission ratio was 5-9x FinAgent's. — [ZeroHedge summary of Bloomberg](https://www.zerohedge.com/markets/wall-street-keeps-testing-ai-traders-most-are-still-underperforming); [TradingAgentStack (vendor)](https://tradingagentstack.com/alpha-arena-results/); [HKU report](https://www.hkubs.hku.hk/aimodelrankings_en/report/En_Agentic%20Trader%20Short%20Report.pdf); [arXiv 2505.07078v4](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.07078v4)
- **Speed.** News is impounded fast: the initial reaction is non-tradable, an intraday entry is modelled 15 minutes after release, and the residual drift is gone in ~2 days; at the high-frequency end each millisecond has measurable value. — [arXiv 2304.07619v6](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2304.07619v6); [NBER w30366](https://www.nber.org/papers/w30366)
- **Running cost is a recognised but under-reported issue.** FINSABER notes "large-scale LLM backtesting is financially intensive" and calls for API costs to be included in evaluations. — [arXiv 2505.07078v4](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.07078v4)

### Inferences
- An LLM bot cannot be validated the way the reader validated rule-based bots: any historical replay inside the model's training window is contaminated. The only clean test is forward paper trading, and the live benchmarks show that 2-10 weeks of forward results cannot distinguish skill from luck.
- If an LLM reads web/news text and can place orders, the news feed is an attack surface. For a thin or small-cap name, a single fabricated post could trigger a trade. Mitigations implied by the research: restrict inputs to a few primary sources, strip non-visible characters/HTML, require confirmation from more than one source, and never let the LLM's text output map directly to an order without rule-based limits.
- Constrain outputs to a fixed schema, compute all sizes and arithmetic in code, set temperature low and/or take a majority of 3-5 runs for classifications; log every prompt and response.
- Polling every few minutes is the costly way to use an LLM on a $1,000 account. Arithmetic: one call every 5 minutes over a 6.5-hour session is 78 calls a day, about 1,600 a month. Even if each call is cheap, the benchmark evidence says more decisions produced more trades, more fees and worse results, not better ones. Event-driven calls (on a new headline or filing) fit the evidence better.

### Gaps
- No source measured LLM API latency or dollar cost per decision against trading outcomes; I did not retrieve current price lists, so no dollar figure is given for the "every few minutes" plan.
- The adversarial-news results are laboratory simulations; I found no documented real-world case of a retail LLM bot being exploited through fake news.
- "Hallucinated figures" specifically (invented prices/fundamentals) were not quantified in the sources read; the documented errors are arithmetic and schema errors. StockBench's summary notes hallucination was not discussed.
- The interaction between a small account's constraints (pattern-day-trader rule, settlement, spreads on small caps) and LLM trading frequency is not covered by any benchmark found.
