# Build the bot, benchmark against SPY

A trader with about $1,000 can build, paper-trade and host a US stock bot for almost nothing, but no strategy family in the evidence reliably beats the bot's honest benchmark: a fractional share of SPY bought once and held. Moving from a thin crypto token to liquid US stocks cuts a round trip from roughly 1.4% to about **0.05% in S&P 500 stocks and under 0.01% in SPY or QQQ**, so cost stops being the reason "buy the dip, sell the spike" fails; the new reason is time spent in cash while the index drifts upward. The largest test of dip-buying (196 rules on the S&P 500, 1965 to 2025) trails buy-and-hold before costs, opening-range breakout and intraday momentum net to about zero after spread and slippage, machine learning needs thousands of stocks and a short book, and no live LLM trader has shown a consistent edge; only trend following has a multi-decade real-money record, and it is modest (about 4% a year over cash). The workable stack is Alpaca's free paper-trading API and IEX data, Lumibot or plain Python, and a $4 to $6 monthly server. As of November 2026 the $25,000 pattern-day-trader rule is gone (replaced on 4 June 2026), yet an account under $2,000 still has no leverage or shorting, T+1 settlement limits a cash account to about one full round trip a day, bot profits are taxed as ordinary income, and the wash-sale rule defers most losses on a ticker the bot keeps re-buying. A cheap LLM earns its place as a schema-bound news reader that can only veto or down-weight a rule-based signal, at roughly **$3 to $50 a year** on Claude Haiku 5.5, and not as a price forecaster polled around the clock. Because LLMs remember history, their backtests are contaminated: the honest test is forward paper trading with every verdict logged, scored against the same bot without the LLM, a random-veto control and buy-and-hold.

## Dip-buying trails the index and only trend following has a real-money record

On a token with no reliable drift, "did it make money" is a fair test. On US stocks it is not: the S&P 500 returned **+255.4% (13.5% a year) from October 2016 to October 2026** ([Backtrex](https://backtrex.com/en/backtests/connors-rsi-2-sp-500)), so a bot that sits in cash between signals has to earn the missed drift back through timing. The table grades each strategy family against that hurdle; this section and the next give the evidence behind each row.

| Strategy family | Best evidence found | What it shows | Practical at $1,000 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Dip-buying an index over weeks to months | Backtest of 196 variants, 1965–2025 | Trails buy-and-hold by about 1.1% a year before costs | Yes, expect to lag SPY |
| Short-term dip rules (RSI(2), IBS) | One vendor run after publication, 2016–2026 | 1.7% a year against 13.5% for holding | Yes: 5 to 10 round trips a year, needs execution near the close |
| Monthly trend rule on ETFs | Post-publication simulation plus live funds | Risk control survived, return halved, live funds lagged the S&P 500 | Yes: a handful of trades a year |
| Diversified trend following (managed futures) | Real money since 2000 | About 4% a year over cash, Sharpe 0.30, frequent 10%+ drawdowns | Only by buying an ETF |
| Opening-range breakout, intraday momentum | Independent re-tests, 2015–2026 | Gross pattern real, net about zero | Long-only and unleveraged; published versions need 4x leverage |
| Stock momentum | Post-publication factor data, one live ETF | Works as a long-only ETF tilt | Only by buying an ETF |
| Machine-learning return prediction | 30-year academic out-of-sample test, before costs | Tiny per-stock edge that needs thousands of stocks and shorting | No |
| LLM trading agents | Live tests of 2 to 10 weeks | No consistent edge, winners rotate | As a text reader, not as a trader |

### Buying dips lost to holding across 60 years of S&P 500 data

The largest systematic test comes from AQR in December 2025: 196 buy-the-dip rules on the S&P 500 (four dip depths, seven dip lengths, seven holding periods) from 1965 to September 2025, in cash when no signal is active. Its answer to whether dip-buying beats buy-and-hold is "The short answer is no": **more than 60% of variants underperformed, the average Sharpe ratio (return per unit of volatility) was 16% below the index, and passive equities earned 1.1% a year more** than the average dip rule. Measured as alpha after adjusting for market exposure, the average rule earned 0.5% a year; only 16 of 196 cleared the ordinary significance bar and none cleared the bar adjusted for having tested 196 ideas, a t-statistic of about 3.5. The mechanism matters for anyone whose core idea is dip-buying. AQR calls it "value investing at a momentum horizon", a bet on reversal over the weeks and months when markets tend to trend, and the rules carried an average market beta of 0.41, so much of what a backtest shows as profit is the index's own drift earned while invested. Two caveats cut in opposite directions: AQR sells trend-following funds, and it modelled no trading costs and tested nothing shorter than one week ([AQR](https://www.aqr.com/-/media/AQR/Documents/Alternative-Thinking/AQR-Alternative-Thinking---Hold-the-Dip.pdf?sc_lang=en)).

The very short-horizon rules that retail bots favour, such as RSI(2) and internal bar strength (IBS), rest on thinner evidence. The published RSI(2) results are hypothetical backtests without transaction costs or data-mining corrections ([CXO Advisory](https://cxoadvisory.com/technical-trading/a-few-notes-on-short-term-trading-strategies-that-work)), and the research found no rigorous study that splits performance into before and after publication. The one ten-year post-publication run located is a single vendor demonstration on an S&P 500 price series that includes a short side, so it needs reproducing on SPY. It shows the classic Connors rule making **73 trades from October 2016 to October 2026 with a 71% win rate and a 1.7% annual return, against 13.5% for buy-and-hold**; the average loss (2.10%) was nearly twice the average win (1.22%) ([Backtrex](https://backtrex.com/en/backtests/connors-rsi-2-sp-500)). Short-term index reversal has a structural basis: index returns have been negatively autocorrelated since the 2000s, tied to the growth of index products ([Baltussen, van Bekkum & Da](https://www3.nd.edu/~zda/Indexing.pdf)). The effect is fragile at the point of execution, though. One IBS study on country ETFs reports, for one basket and in sample, a Sharpe ratio of 3.73 when trading at the same close the signal is computed from and −0.25 when trading open to open ([arXiv 2306.12434](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.12434)).

Two lessons carry straight into the trader's own testing habit. A rule that trades about seven times a year cannot be judged on one unseen month, which will contain zero to two trades; it needs years of sealed data. And the benchmark has to be exposure-adjusted: a dip bot that carries 40% of the index's risk and earns 40% of its return has shown no timing skill.

### Trend rules kept their risk control and lost half their return

Trend following is the only family here with a multi-decade real-money record. The SG Trend Index, which tracks the ten largest trend-following funds net of fees, earned **4.0% a year over cash from 2000 to September 2025 at a Sharpe ratio of 0.30** ([AQR](https://www.aqr.com/-/media/AQR/Documents/Alternative-Thinking/AQR-Alternative-Thinking---Hold-the-Dip.pdf?sc_lang=en)), and in the four worst S&P 500 drawdowns since 2000 it averaged a gain while the average dip rule lost money ([Alpha Architect](https://alphaarchitect.com/is-trend-following-better-than-buy-the-dip/)). The ride is rough: +27.4% in 2022, then a 20.4% fall from May 2024 to May 2025, with 16 drawdowns over 10% since 2000 ([Cambridge Associates](https://publishedresearch.cambridgeassociates.com/?p=19140)). In 2026 the managed-futures ETFs DBMF and KMLM were each up about 15% by early November ([Total Real Returns](https://totalrealreturns.com/n/KMLM,DBMF,SPY)) against 6.98% for the S&P 500 on 18 November ([Dow Jones via Morningstar](https://www.morningstar.com/news/dow-jones/202611188038/sp-500-falls-023-to-732363-data-talk)). A $1,000 account reaches this strategy only by buying such an ETF at fees of 0.85% to 0.90% ([24/7 Wall St](https://247wallst.com/investing/2026/04/10/the-3-managed-futures-etfs-that-made-money-while-the-sp-500-crashed/)), which is a holding decision, not a bot.

The simple version a bot can run, holding an ETF only while its price is above a 10-month or 200-day average, has survived publication as a drawdown reducer and not as a return enhancer. Faber's five-asset timing model went from a backtested Sharpe of 0.81 and an 11.7% annual return (1972–2005) to 0.68 and 6.05% in the nineteen years after, in a simulation by a firm that sells trading research ([Concretum](https://concretumgroup.substack.com/p/global-tactical-asset-allocation)). That halving matches the wider finding that published stock-market predictors lose 58% of their return after publication ([McLean & Pontiff](https://www.gwern.net/doc/economics/2016-mclean.pdf)). Real funds built on such rules did worse than the simulations: Cambria's Global Momentum ETF returned about 3.5% a year over ten years against about 11.2% for the S&P 500, on an undated third-party figure ([PortfoliosLab](https://portfolioslab.com/tools/stock-comparison/GMOM/SPY)), and Morningstar's verdict on Pacer's Trendpilot large-cap fund is that it "has tried to time the market. It hasn't gone well" ([Morningstar](https://www.morningstar.com/funds/this-fund-followed-rules-that-was-problem)). Classic stock momentum needs dozens of positions and a short side. Its only practical form at $1,000 is a long-only ETF such as MTUM, which returned 391% against 325% for SPY over the ten years to June 2026 ([24/7 Wall St](https://247wallst.com/investing/2026/06/17/forget-the-plain-sp-500-the-momentum-version-captured-the-run-and-beat-it-by-15-this-year/)) but is close to a cap-weighted mega-cap portfolio and not the academic factor ([Alpha Architect](https://alphaarchitect.com/momentum-and-size/)).

### Opening-range breakout reproduces gross and nets to zero

The best-known recent claim for retail day trading is the opening-range breakout (ORB) on QQQ: trade in the direction of the first five-minute candle with a stop at its other side. The paper reports a 33.2% annual return from 2016 to February 2023 against 14.9% for holding QQQ, and assumes a $25,000 account, up to 4x leverage, zero slippage and no bid-ask spread ([CXO Advisory](https://www.cxoadvisory.com/technical-trading/day-trading-with-an-opening-range-breakout-strategy)). An independent replication of the unchanged rule on five index CFDs from 2015 to June 2026 found the pattern real before costs (+0.131 of risk per trade on the Nasdaq, t = 2.9) and gone after them: **+0.002 on the Nasdaq and negative on the other four indices once spread and slippage are charged**, because costs ran at 6% to 12% of risk on every trade. Its verdict: "The paper is right about the pattern and wrong about the trade" ([Krueger, MQL5](https://www.mql5.com/en/blogs/post/776235)). A second re-test on QQQ put the edge at $0.04 per trade and found that six cents of total slippage turned it into a loss ([Girlando](https://portfoliolucagirlando.odoo.com/p16)). Neither replication is peer-reviewed and the first uses CFD data, but they agree with each other and with the other intraday claims. The peer-reviewed finding that the first half-hour's return predicts the last half-hour's ([Gao, Han, Li & Zhou](https://profiles.wustl.edu/en/publications/market-intraday-momentum/)) produced a Sharpe ratio of −0.63 in a 2015–2020 re-test ([QuantConnect](https://www.quantconnect.com/research/15348/intraday-etf-momentum/p1)), and the only funds built on the overnight-return anomaly closed after about a year ([Elm Wealth](https://elmwealth.com/night-shift/)). This is the crypto failure in miniature: an edge the same size as the cost of collecting it.

### Persistence makes day traders' odds worse

Complete account records give the base rate. Of 19,646 Brazilians who started day trading index futures in 2013–2015, the share in profit fell as persistence rose, and of the 1,551 who lasted more than 300 days, 97% lost money ([Quantpedia](https://quantpedia.com/retail-day-trading-is-an-uphill-battle/)). Fewer than 1% of Taiwanese day traders earned reliable profits net of fees over 15 years ([Barber, Lee, Liu & Odean](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/finmar/v18y2014icp1-24.html)), and more than 70% of Indian intraday equity traders lost money in fiscal 2023 ([Business Standard](https://www.business-standard.com/markets/news/over-70-intra-day-traders-incur-losses-during-fy23-reveals-sebi-study-124072401110_1.html)). Automation does not change the base rate by itself. Across 888 algorithms on the Quantopian platform, **backtest Sharpe explained only 1% to 2% of out-of-sample performance, while backtest volatility and drawdown carried over far better** (R-squared of 0.67 and 0.34) ([Quantpedia](https://quantpedia.com/quantopians-academic-paper-about-in-vs-out-of-sample-performance-of-trading-alg/)). A backtest is a decent guide to how bad things can get and a poor guide to how much a bot will make. Professionals fare similarly: the median rule-based strategy sold by banks went from a backtested Sharpe of 1.20 to 0.31 live ([CXO Advisory](https://www.cxoadvisory.com/big-ideas/live-performance-of-alternative-beta-products/)), and 79% of active US large-cap funds trailed the S&P 500 in 2025 ([InvestmentNews](https://www.investmentnews.com/equities/active-managers-stumble-again-in-2025-as-large-caps-dominate/265541)). The working prior that follows from these decay figures is about half the return of an honest out-of-sample backtest, and a quarter to a third of an optimised one.

## Machine learning and LLM traders show no edge a $1,000 account can harvest

### The best ML models explain 0.4% of a stock's monthly move

Machine learning does predict stock returns better than simple regression, and the size of the improvement is the whole story. The benchmark study trained on nearly 30,000 stocks over 60 years with more than 900 predictors and tested on 1987–2016. The best neural network explained **0.40% of the variation in monthly stock-level returns out of sample, a three-variable linear model 0.16%, and an unregularised regression on all predictors −3.46%**, worse than forecasting zero. That sliver becomes a Sharpe ratio of 1.35 only through a long-short portfolio across thousands of stocks with 110% to 130% monthly turnover, before trading costs. The models found no hidden pattern: they re-weighted known signals, chiefly recent price trend, liquidity and volatility ([Gu, Kelly & Xiu](https://www.nber.org/papers/w25398.pdf)). Removing microcaps cut the profit by 62% and removing distressed firms by 80%, and a co-author of that critique concluded that "an average investor would struggle to achieve alpha after taking transaction costs into account" ([CUHK Business School](https://cbk.bschool.cuhk.edu.hk/the-limitations-of-using-ai-to-pick-stocks/)). Data scale is decisive. A "complex" model fitted on windows as short as 12 months learns nothing beyond a volatility-timed momentum rule, and builds the same rule even when fed simulated data that contains reversals ([Nagel](https://www.nber.org/papers/w34104)). A model fitted to a few months of one instrument sits far below the scale at which ML has been shown to work. For a trader who wants an ML model anyway, the evidence favours a small, regularised linear model with a few inputs chosen in advance; feeding many indicators into a plain regression is the worst case in the study.

### Forty-five tried settings can fool a five-year backtest

The trader's crypto experience, rules that shone in backtests and failed on unseen data, is the outcome the overfitting literature predicts. With five years of data, "one can be misled by looking at even as few as 45 sample configurations": among that many variants at least one will look good in sample and fail out of sample ([Bailey, Borwein, López de Prado & Zhu](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/817304)). The probability that a selected backtest is overfit rises with the number of configurations tried ([SSRN 2326253](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=2326253)), which is why a new finding in finance should clear a t-statistic of about 3.0 instead of 2.0 ([Harvey, Liu & Zhu](https://www.nber.org/papers/w20592)) and why the deflated Sharpe ratio discounts a result for the number of trials behind it ([Bailey & López de Prado](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=2460551)). Stocks offer decades of data in place of five months, which makes genuine out-of-sample testing possible, but more data does not cure selection: AQR's 196 dip rules had 60 years to work with and none passed. Switching from rules to ML raises the risk because ML has more free parameters, and an LLM asked to "find a strategy" by iterating on backtests is a multiple-testing machine. Man Group describes exactly this problem in its own AI research agents ([Man Group](https://www.man.com/insights/what-ai-can-do-for-alpha)). The discipline that follows is simple and tedious: count every variant tried, keep a block of sealed data that is opened once, and treat the best of a thousand backtests as unremarkable.

### LLMs read headlines well, but the tradable part dies at 20 basis points

The strongest LLM result is about reading text. On headlines for 4,123 US stocks dated after the model's knowledge cutoff (October 2021 to May 2024), GPT-4 called the direction of the market's first reaction about 90% of the time, a move nobody can trade, and its scores also predicted the drift over the following two days, giving a long-short Sharpe ratio near 3 before costs. Everything about harvesting it is institutional. Most of the profit came from the short leg (26 basis points a day against 8 for the long leg), turnover ran about 190% a day, **a 20-basis-point round-trip cost made the strategy unprofitable, and its Sharpe ratio fell from 6.54 in late 2021 to 1.22 by early 2024** as LLM use spread ([Lopez-Lira & Tang](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2304.07619v6)). The sample ends in May 2024; whether any drift remains is unknown.

Backtests of LLM trading agents are a different matter, because the model may already know what happened. LLMs can reproduce market values from before their training cutoff and nothing after it ([Lopez-Lira, Tang & Zhu](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.14765v2)). Published agents lose about half their Sharpe ratio once the test moves past the cutoff ([Profit Mirage](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.07920v1)). Over 2004–2024 with delisted stocks included, buy-and-hold beat both leading LLM agents, whose claimed edge the authors call "a methodological artefact of narrow, biased evaluations" ([FINSABER](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.07078v4)). Leakage is not the whole story for news, since at least 54% of news-based predictability survives in models trained only on text available at each date ([He et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.11677v1)), but for a current model it rules out essentially all the history a retail trader could replay.

### In live contests the winners rotate and most accounts lose

Forward tests, the only clean kind, are short and unflattering. In the one real-money contest on US stock exposure, Alpha Arena Season 1.5 (about two weeks from 19 November 2025, 32 accounts of $10,000, reported by a third party to have traded leveraged stock perpetuals instead of cash shares), five or six accounts finished in profit and the group lost roughly a third of its capital; under one prompt, one model placed 1,418 trades and another 158 ([ForkLog](https://forklog.com/en/ai-model-grok-4-2-triumphs-in-trading-tournament/); [ZeroHedge summary of Bloomberg](https://www.zerohedge.com/markets/wall-street-keeps-testing-ai-traders-most-are-still-underperforming); [TradingAgentStack](https://tradingagentstack.com/alpha-arena-results/)). These figures come from press and vendor recomputations that disagree in places, because the organiser published no benchmark or methodology. In unleveraged simulated-money tests the models behaved like slightly under-invested index holders. All 21 models in LiveTradeBench gained between 1.78% and 6.25% over 50 trading days, with a correlation of 0.054 between a model's general-capability score and its return ([LiveTradeBench](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.03628v1)). Three of six beat QQQ over five weeks in AI-Trader, with cheaper models on top and GPT-5 below the index ([AI-Trader](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.10971v1)). In StockBench "most LLM agents struggle to outperform the simple buy-and-hold baseline" ([StockBench](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.02209v2)). Bloomberg's May 2026 verdict on the public arenas, of which only two of eleven had a profitable median model: "Most of the systems lose money. They trade too much. They make wildly different decisions when given identical instructions" ([ZeroHedge summary of Bloomberg](https://www.zerohedge.com/markets/wall-street-keeps-testing-ai-traders-most-are-still-underperforming)). Alpha Arena's founder gave the reason, as quoted by a vendor blog: "LLMs don't really handle numerical time series data very well" ([Thor](https://thortradecopier.com/blog/can-chatgpt-trade-for-you)).

Two findings shape the design in the final section. Model price buys nothing when the job is deciding trades: cheap and flagship models finish above and below each other more or less at random. And where LLMs are reliable they are also repeatable: over 50 repeated runs, sentiment classification reached agreement scores above 0.94, while numerical predictions were less stable ([Wang & Wang](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.16974)).

## A round trip costs about 0.05%, so fixed fees become the real drag

### Spreads in liquid names are 30 to 300 times cheaper than the token

At a commission-free broker the cost of trading is the bid-ask spread plus a small regulatory fee on sales. A study of all SEC execution-quality reports from January 2019 to March 2022 measured what retail market orders paid at the wholesalers that fill them, expressed as a full buy-and-sell round trip ([Dyhrberg, Shkilko & Werner](https://conference.nber.org/conf_papers/f187847/f187847.pdf)). The SPY and QQQ row uses quoted spreads from fund-data sources ([Trackinsight](https://www.trackinsight.com/en/compare-etfs/SPY,QQQ); [State Street](https://www.ssga.com/us/en/individual/insights/comparing-spy-and-spym-two-state-street-etfs-for-s-p-500-exposure)), and the dollar column is arithmetic on the cited rates.

| What is traded | Round-trip spread cost | On a $1,000 trade |
|---|---|---|
| SPY or QQQ | about 0.003% to 0.005% | about $0.03 to $0.05 |
| S&P 500 stocks | 0.05% (4.62 basis points) | $0.46 |
| Other stocks, largest third | 0.16% | $1.60 |
| Other stocks, middle third | 0.43% | $4.28 |
| Other stocks, smallest third | 0.94% | $9.36 |
| The thin crypto token | about 1.4% | $14.00 |

On top sits the SEC fee of $20.60 per million dollars sold, two cents on a $1,000 sale ([SEC](https://www.sec.gov/rules-regulations/fee-rate-advisories/2026-2)); FINRA's per-share trading activity fee is paused from 1 October to 31 December 2026 ([FINRA](https://www.finra.org/rules-guidance/rule-filings/sr-finra-2026-021)). A dip-and-spike bot that needed moves above 1.4% just to break even needs almost nothing on SPY, and it gets most of the token's cost problem back the moment it trades small caps. With spreads this small, fixed dollar charges dominate a $1,000 account. A $10 monthly subscription is 1% of the account every month, and Interactive Brokers' $1.00 minimum commission is 1% of a $100 order each way ([Interactive Brokers](https://investors.interactivebrokers.com/en/pricing/commissions-home.php)).

Alpaca's paper engine fills orders only when they are marketable against live quotes, but it ignores quoted size, queue position for resting limit orders, latency, regulatory fees and dividends, and paper-only accounts see quotes from a single exchange, IEX ([Alpaca](https://docs.alpaca.markets/docs/paper-trading)). Paper results therefore overstate live results, most of all for strategies that rest limit orders or depend on tight spreads. Alpaca is paid for order flow by the wholesalers it routes to ([Alpaca Rule 606 report](https://files.alpaca.markets/disclosures/library/SEC+606a1+-+2026Q2.pdf)), and its status page logged several 2026 incidents, including orders intermittently not filling and a pause in overnight trading ([Alpaca status](https://status.alpaca.markets/)). Those are reasons for the bot to handle rejections and outages instead of assuming fills. The recommendation is a judgement about fit for a no-deposit start, not a claim that Alpaca executes better.

### Free data covers ETFs; the traps are adjustments and dead tickers

A free Alpaca account supplies minute bars back to 2016 and real-time IEX quotes; consolidated all-exchange data costs $99 a month ([Alpaca](https://docs.alpaca.markets/docs/about-market-data-api)). The yfinance library adds free daily history but only the last seven days of one-minute bars ([yfinance](https://github.com/ranaroussi/yfinance/wiki/Ticker)). Two traps matter more than price. Alpaca bars are unadjusted by default, so a backtest that forgets the adjustment setting reads a stock split as a crash ([Alpaca](https://docs.alpaca.markets/us/reference/stockbars)), and users have reported wrong split ratios ([Alpaca forum](https://forum.alpaca.markets/t/incorrect-historical-data-of-stock-split-ratios/17839)), so adjusted prices deserve a spot-check against a second source. And any free list of currently trading tickers silently omits the companies that died, which inflates stock-picking backtests: most US stocks since 1926 returned less than Treasury bills over their lives, and all net wealth creation came from about 4% of them ([Bessembinder](https://news.wpcarey.asu.edu/20180917-do-stocks-outperform-treasury-bills)). History that includes delisted names costs money, for example FirstRate Data's archive of 16,322 tickers with more than 7,000 delisted ([FirstRate Data](https://firstratedata.com/stock-data)) or Databento at $199 a month ([Databento](https://databento.com/blog/upcoming-changes-to-pricing-plans-in-january-2025)). A bot that trades a fixed handful of large ETFs sidesteps the problem, which is one more argument for starting there.

### Lumibot and a $4 server complete a near-free stack

Among open-source Python frameworks, Lumibot is the direct fit: native Alpaca support, the same strategy class for backtest and for paper or live trading ([Lumibot](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Lumiwealth/lumibot/dev/README.md)), and 201 releases in 2026, which is a reason to pin the version ([PyPI](https://pypi.org/project/lumibot/)). QuantConnect's LEAN supports the most brokers, but its cloud needs the $84-a-month tier for any paper or live deployment ([QuantConnect](https://www.quantconnect.com/pricing)). backtrader has had no release since April 2023 ([PyPI](https://pypi.org/project/backtrader/)); vectorbt, bt, Backtesting.py and zipline-reloaded are backtest-only; NautilusTrader's only stock broker is Interactive Brokers ([NautilusTrader](https://nautilustrader.io/docs/latest/integrations/)); and Freqtrade remains crypto-only ([PyPI](https://pypi.org/project/freqtrade/)). Whatever the tool, the look-ahead switch to watch is filling at the same bar's close. Backtesting.py and backtrader fill at the next bar's open by default, and `trade_on_close=True` or cheat-on-close removes that protection ([Backtesting.py](https://kernc.github.io/backtesting.py/doc/backtesting/backtesting.html); [backtrader](https://www.backtrader.com/docu/broker/)), which is precisely the assumption the IBS results above depend on.

Hosting is the last fixed cost. A bot acting on five-minute-or-slower bars runs on a $4 or $6 DigitalOcean droplet ([DigitalOcean](https://www.digitalocean.com/pricing/droplets)) or Google Cloud's free e2-micro instance ([Google Cloud](https://docs.cloud.google.com/free/docs/free-cloud-features)), with a free heartbeat monitor so a silent crash is noticed ([Healthchecks.io](https://healthchecks.io/pricing/)). Oracle's free tier reclaims instances it deems idle, which is the profile of a quiet bot ([Oracle](https://docs.oracle.com/en-us/iaas/Content/FreeTier/resourceref.htm)). GitHub Actions is unsuitable for the loop itself, with a five-minute minimum interval, delays under load and dropped runs ([GitHub](https://docs.github.com/en/actions/reference/workflows-and-actions/events-that-trigger-workflows)), and Claude Code's cloud scheduled tasks have a one-hour minimum ([Claude Code docs](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/scheduled-tasks)). Even $6 a month is 0.6% of the account, more than the trading cost of dozens of ETF round trips, so a home computer is a reasonable start during paper trading.

## The $25,000 day-trading rule is gone, but $2,000, T+1 and wash sales still bind

This section is background research as of 23 November 2026, not legal or tax advice. Several broker-specific details came from search snippets and need checking with the broker.

### Day trading is open to small accounts; leverage and shorting are not

The SEC approved FINRA's replacement of the pattern-day-trader rule on 14 April 2026 and it took effect on **4 June 2026**: the four-day-trades-in-five-days count and the $25,000 minimum are both eliminated and replaced by intraday margin standards ([Federal Register](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2026-04-17/html/2026-07485.htm); [FINRA Regulatory Notice 26-10](https://www.finra.org/rules-guidance/notices/26-10)). Two qualifications matter. Brokers that need more time may keep the old rules until 20 October 2027 and may impose stricter house rules at any time, so the regime must be confirmed broker by broker. And the $2,000 floor for borrowing survives: "You can trade in a margin account with less than $2,000 in equity, but you cannot use leverage" ([FINRA](https://www.finra.org/investors/insights/intraday-margin-requirements)). Alpaca lifted the rule for API users on 4 June 2026 and removed the old API fields (`pattern_day_trader`, `daytrade_count`, `daytrading_buying_power`) by 6 July 2026, so bot code copied from older tutorials can break ([Alpaca](https://alpaca.markets/blog/finra-retires-the-pdt-rule-introducing-alpacas-new-intraday-margin-framework/)). Its accounts under $2,000 get 1x buying power and no short selling ([Alpaca docs](https://docs.alpaca.markets/us/docs/margin-and-short-selling)), so for this account "sell the spike" can only mean selling shares already owned. Given the loss statistics above, the rule change removes a protection more than it adds an opportunity; the SEC itself noted that technological advances "do not mitigate the risk inherent in day trading" ([Federal Register](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2026-04-17/html/2026-07485.htm)).

Account type sets how often the money can turn over. In a cash account, stock trades settle the next business day, T+1 since 28 May 2024 ([SEC](https://www.sec.gov/news/press-release/2023-29)), and selling a position bought with unsettled proceeds is a good-faith violation; three in twelve months, or one freeriding violation, restricts the account to settled-cash purchases for 90 days ([Fidelity](https://www.fidelity.com/learning-center/trading-investing/trading/avoiding-cash-trading-violations)). Working through those rules, each dollar funds about **one buy-and-sell round trip per trading day**, and a bot that ignores settlement can collect three violations in an afternoon. A margin account holding less than $2,000 avoids the settlement wait without granting leverage, as Alpaca documents it ([Alpaca docs](https://docs.alpaca.markets/us/docs/intraday-margin-rule-for-non-leverage-margin-accounts)); other brokers' treatment needs confirming.

### Wash sales defer nearly every loss on a one-ticker bot

Every profitable sale of a stock held a year or less is a short-term gain taxed as ordinary income ([IRS Topic 409](https://www.irs.gov/taxtopics/tc409)), at 2026 federal rates of 10% to 37%, whereas gains held over a year pay 0% up to $49,450 of taxable income for a single filer and 15% from there to $545,500 ([Tax Foundation](https://taxfoundation.org/data/all/federal/2026-tax-brackets/)). Net capital losses offset other income only up to $3,000 a year. The rule that bites a dip bot is the wash sale: a loss is disallowed if substantially identical stock is bought within 30 days before or after the sale, and the loss is added to the cost basis of the replacement shares. The broker's Form 1099-B flags only same-account, same-security cases, yet "you cannot deduct a loss from a wash sale even if it is not reported on Form 1099-B" ([IRS Publication 550](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p550)). A bot cycling one ticker therefore has nearly every loss deferred until it finally sells out and stays out for 31 days. Two hazards follow from the IRS text. Losses realised in December and re-bought in January move into the next tax year, so a year's taxable gain can exceed the account's actual profit. And a loss is lost for good if the replacement purchase happens in an IRA. At $1,000 the dollars are small and the record-keeping is not.

Trader tax status with a Section 475(f) mark-to-market election switches the wash-sale rule off, but it requires substantial, continuous and regular trading, and the election must be filed by the due date of the prior year's return, which for tax year 2027 means mid-April 2027 ([IRS Topic 429](https://www.irs.gov/taxtopics/tc429)). It is an unrealistic fit for a part-time $1,000 account. One real difference from the crypto practice runs: as of November 2026 the wash-sale rule does not apply to digital assets ([Congressional Research Service](https://www.everycrsreport.com/reports/IF13359.html)), though a bill extending it cleared the House Ways and Means Committee 38–5 in September 2026 ([EY](https://taxnews.ey.com/news/2026-1972-ways-and-means-approves-digital-assets-tax-bill)) with no further action reported.

### A bot needs no licence, and the manipulation rules apply to it unchanged

Individuals who buy and sell securities for their own account are traders, not dealers, and need no registration ([SEC](https://www.sec.gov/about/reports-publications/investor-publications/guide-broker-dealer-registration)). The anti-manipulation laws apply to a script exactly as to a person: the SEC sanctioned an individual day trader with a $50,000 penalty and $140,250 of disgorgement for spoofing, placing orders he did not intend to execute ([SEC](https://www.sec.gov/enforce/34-89517-s)). A simple bot that only sends orders it wants filled is not doing that. Its realistic accident is crossing its own orders, which Alpaca rejects with an HTTP 403 error that the bot must handle ([Alpaca](https://docs.alpaca.markets/us/v1.4.2/docs/user-protection)). No regulator has issued a rule specific to retail AI trading. After Robinhood opened accounts to AI agents in May 2026 with a disclosure that it "does not control, supervise, monitor, recommend, or audit these AI agents" ([Robinhood](https://robinhood.com/us/en/newsroom/robinhood-is-now-open-to-agents/)), six members of Congress asked the SEC in June 2026 what guardrails it requires ([Foster and Sherman letter](https://foster.house.gov/sites/evo-subsites/foster-evo.house.gov/files/evo-media-document/foster_sherman-request-for-information-re-agentic-trading-6.23.2026.pdf)); the research found no SEC reply.

### Overnight sessions are thin, limit-only and unprotected by price bands

Retail overnight trading already runs through off-exchange venues, and Nasdaq's own night session (9 PM to 4 AM Eastern) "is expected to launch" on Sunday 6 December 2026 ([Nasdaq](https://www.nasdaqtrader.com/tradernews.aspx?id=dn2026-8)), a date still described as pending. Overnight trading was about 0.9% of US share volume in August 2026 ([Databento](https://databento.com/blog/us-equities-overnight-trading)); at Alpaca it accepts limit orders only, and a halt typically lasts the whole session ([Alpaca](https://docs.alpaca.markets/us/docs/245-trading)). The single-stock price bands that pause trading for five minutes after a sharp move operate only from 9:30 AM to 4:00 PM Eastern, and market-wide halts trigger at 7%, 13% and 20% falls in the S&P 500 ([FINRA](https://www.finra.org/investors/insights/guardrails-market-volatility)). A halt is the worst moment for a naive bot, since orders sit unfilled and a stop-loss guarantees no exit price, so trading regular hours only is the sensible default. SIPC covers up to $500,000 if the broker fails and assets are missing; it never covers trading losses ([SIPC](https://www.sipc.org/for-investors/what-sipc-protects)).

## A cheap LLM belongs in the loop as a news veto costing dollars a year

### Code computes every number; the model only labels text

The systems that exist share one division of labour. The well-known open-source projects are research or teaching frameworks with simulated execution: TradingAgents "is designed for research purposes" and sends approved orders to a simulated exchange ([TradingAgents](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/TauricResearch/TradingAgents/main/README.md)), and ai-hedge-fund states that "the system does not actually make any trades" ([ai-hedge-fund](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/virattt/ai-hedge-fund/main/README.md)). The cleanest published build, on Alpaca's own site, has LLM agents return structured proposals while position caps, drawdown halts (5% daily, 10% weekly, 15% total) and broker-side stop orders run as "deterministic code, unit-tested, with no model in the loop", and a human approves each proposal. Its paper results, 25 closed trades at about a 48% win rate, are too few to mean anything, as its author says ([Alpaca Learn](https://alpaca.markets/learn/building-a-multi-agent-ai-trading-system-on-alpaca)).

Applied to a $1,000 account, the design that fits the evidence gives the LLM one narrow job: read fresh text and return a small, schema-checked verdict (event type, direction, confidence, and the IDs of the headlines it relied on). Code polls prices, computes every indicator and order size, and runs the rule-based bot. The bot's own signal must already want the trade; the LLM can only confirm, down-weight or veto it. This uses the skill with evidence behind it, reading news, and avoids the one without, forecasting from price bars. It also fails safely: a wrong veto costs a skipped trade, whereas an LLM that places orders has unbounded downside from one bad output. No study has tested this veto design directly. It is an inference from the component evidence, which is why the test protocol below matters.

### Around the clock every few minutes is the wrong schedule for stocks

US stocks are not a 24/7 market. There is a 6.5-hour regular session, thinner pre-market and after-hours trading, a limit-only overnight session on weekdays and nothing at weekends ([Alpaca](https://docs.alpaca.markets/docs/245-trading)), and SEC filings submitted after 5:30 PM Eastern are generally published the next business day ([SEC](https://www.sec.gov/search-filings/edgar-search-assistance/accessing-edgar-data)). Price bars arriving every few minutes contain almost no new text, so a clock-driven call mostly re-reads stale input, and in the live contests more decisions produced more trades and worse results. The schedule should follow information instead. Alpaca streams Benzinga-sourced headlines over WebSocket ([Alpaca](https://docs.alpaca.markets/docs/streaming-real-time-news)) and EDGAR serves filings free at up to ten requests a second ([SEC](https://www.sec.gov/about/developer-resources)), so code can wake the model when a watchlist headline or filing lands, add a slow timer (every 15 minutes in regular hours), and run one digest overnight and at weekends. A fixed timer alone misses things: one practitioner's 30-minute poll "missed all 32 signals in one week" before an event-driven monitor replaced it ([DEV Community](https://dev.to/xqliu/26-days-of-ai-trading-14-trades-3-system-rewrites-and-one-expensive-lesson-4ai9)). Treating the overnight session as analyse-only is the conservative default.

### Five-minute calls cost $9 to $47 a year; heavy calls every minute cost more than the account

Claude Haiku 5.5 lists at $0.10 per million input tokens and $0.50 per million output tokens for prompts up to 100,000 tokens, with cache reads at a tenth of the input price and the asynchronous Batch API at half price ([Anthropic](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/about-claude/pricing)). A light call (3,000 tokens in, 300 out) therefore costs $0.00045 and a heavy call that includes news text (20,000 in, 800 out) $0.0024. The table is arithmetic on those list prices with assumed token counts; real prompts should be measured.

| Schedule | Call size | Calls per day | Per month | Per year |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Regular hours, every 15 minutes | Light | 26 | $0.25 | $2.95 |
| Regular hours, every 5 minutes | Light | 78 | $0.74 | $8.85 |
| Regular hours, every 5 minutes | Heavy | 78 | $3.93 | $47.17 |
| Around the clock, every 5 minutes | Light | 288 | $3.94 | $47.30 |
| Around the clock, every 5 minutes | Heavy | 288 | $21.02 | $252.29 |
| Around the clock, every minute | Heavy | 1,440 | $105.12 | $1,261.44 |
| Hybrid: 15-minute timer plus about 23 event or digest calls per trading day | Mixed | about 49 | about $1.40 | about $17 |

Against a $1,000 account the LLM bill is a return hurdle: 0.3% a year for the lightest schedule, 4.7% for light five-minute calls around the clock, and 126% for heavy calls every minute. Other cheap models land within about a factor of two, with GPT-5 nano at $0.05 and $0.40 per million tokens ([OpenAI](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/pricing)) and Gemini 2.5 Flash-Lite at $0.10 and $0.40 ([Google](https://aistudio.google.com/docs/pricing)), a difference of cents a month here, while Claude Sonnet 5.5 at $2 and $10 costs twenty times as much for no demonstrated trading benefit. Three billing details move the numbers. Haiku 5.5 thinks by default and thinking is billed as output. A prompt over 100,000 tokens is repriced at five times the rate for the whole request. And prompt caching saves about 40% at one- to five-minute cadence but costs slightly more than no caching at 15-minute cadence unless the one-hour cache is used ([Anthropic](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/about-claude/pricing)).

### Fake headlines, refusals and runaway loops need code-side controls

LLM bots fail in ways rule bots do not. In a 2026 test across 15 US stocks, fabricated news steered LLM trading agents 62% of the time against 14% for direct prompt injection, and stripping surface credibility cues such as a named source or a "BREAKING" tag cut one attack's success from 100% to 0% ([Fake News, Real Crashes](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.26438)); invisible character swaps in headlines also broke sentiment models ([arXiv 2601.13082](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.13082v1)). These are laboratory results with no documented real-world retail case, but they argue for a short list of trusted sources, Unicode normalisation, and a second source or a matching price move before any headline can influence an order. Repeatability cannot come from sampling settings, because Haiku 5.5 rejects a non-default temperature; it has to come from structured outputs with enumerated fields. The model can also decline a request with an ordinary HTTP 200 and `stop_reason: "refusal"`, so code must check before reading a verdict ([Anthropic](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/about-claude/models/migration-guide)). And the entry API tier permits 4,000 requests a minute under a $500 monthly cap ([Anthropic](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/api/rate-limits)): a looping bug at full throughput would burn about $24 an hour, which makes a workspace spend limit of a few dollars and a code-side daily budget the first things to configure. The governing rule is that every non-answer (error, timeout, refusal, schema failure, stale reply) becomes "no opinion", and the bot must behave correctly with the LLM unplugged. Guardrail code needs testing against the real paper API as well: one practitioner found four bugs that had turned a stop-loss into a plain limit order while the tests passed against mocks ([DEV Community](https://dev.to/xqliu/26-days-of-ai-trading-14-trades-3-system-rewrites-and-one-expensive-lesson-4ai9)).

### The honest test runs forward, in shadow, against a random veto

A rule-based bot can be tested on sealed history; an LLM cannot, because the bias lives in the model's weights. Anonymising company names changes in-sample results ([Glasserman & Lin](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.17322)), models trained only on data up to a fixed date expose the look-ahead bias that standard LLMs carry ([Look-Ahead-Bench](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.13770)), and the clean designs test forward on live data ([LiveTradeBench](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.03628)). For a trader whose bots have always failed on unseen data, that means a test in which unseen data is the only data.

The protocol is to freeze the prompt, schema and model ID and count nothing dated before the freeze or before the model's knowledge cutoff; Haiku 5.5's cutoff was not confirmed in the research and needs checking, and any change restarts the clock. Each verdict is logged when it is made, with the input snapshot, the rule bot's own signal and the reference price, and a nightly job fills in what happened after 15 minutes, an hour and a day. The LLM runs in shadow mode first, recording what its veto would have done without acting on it, and three curves are compared over the same events: the bot alone, the bot with the filter, and buy-and-hold of the same symbols and of SPY. One control separates skill from reduced exposure. If the LLM vetoes 30% of signals, the result of vetoing a random 30% is computed many times over, and the LLM has to beat that distribution. The verdicts are also scored directly (direction hit rate against a coin flip, and whether stated confidence tracks accuracy), the filtered curve is charged the LLM bill and realistic spreads, and the pass mark, the minimum number of scored events and the test length are fixed before starting. With a handful of signals a day this takes months; that is a judgement and not a calculated sample size, but every public LLM contest so far has been called "too short and too noisy" for firm conclusions ([ThinkAdvisor](https://www.thinkadvisor.com/amp/2026/05/06/why-ai-bots-arent-ready-to-replace-your-fund-manager/)).

The same honesty sets the order of building. Paper-hold SPY as the benchmark first. Add one low-turnover rule on SPY or QQQ, an end-of-day dip rule or a monthly trend rule, validated on years of sealed data with every tried variant counted. Run it on Alpaca paper for months, expecting live fills to be worse than paper. Attach the LLM in shadow mode. Only then consider real money, in regular hours, with limit orders and settlement tracked.

## Conclusion

Moving from a thin token to US stocks does not make "buy the dip, sell the spike" work; it changes why it struggles. On the token, costs ate an edge that was probably never there. On SPY, costs vanish and the opponent becomes the index's own drift, which no dip rule overcame in 60 years of data and which a bot forfeits every hour it holds cash. That reframes what the project is for. Backtested risk carries into live trading far better than backtested return, so the realistic product of a well-built bot is controlled drawdown, a trustworthy log and a tested process, not excess return over a fractional share of SPY. Seen that way, the monthly trend rule and the managed-futures ETF are tools for shaping risk, and the intraday strategies newly opened to small accounts by the June 2026 rule change are an invitation to the activity with the worst base rate in the evidence.

The LLM question resolves the same way. Its documented skill, reading text, is being competed away as adoption rises, and its documented weakness, trading from numbers, shows up in every live contest. What remains for a small trader is cheap hygiene: a veto on fresh bad news, summaries, decision logs, and review of the bot's own code for look-ahead and multiple-testing errors. Whether a cheap model's veto improves a rule-based stock bot is an open question that no published study has tested. A forward shadow log costing a few dollars a month would be the first direct evidence, and it is the rare experiment in this field whose honest result, including "no difference", the trader can obtain alone. Three dates bear watching: the exchange night session expected on 6 December 2026, each broker's switch to the new intraday margin rules before October 2027, and the pending bill that would extend wash-sale rules to crypto.
